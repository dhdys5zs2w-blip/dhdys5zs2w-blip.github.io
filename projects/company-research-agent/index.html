A due-diligence method that refuses to anchor

Research on a private company usually fails the same way: someone finds a plausible number early, it becomes the anchor, and everything after is arranged to support it. I designed a method to interrupt that at every step and packaged it as a Claude Code workflow: give it a company name and it produces a three-part dossier and a financial model from public filings and patents, with the bear case written before the verdict.

What it does

Say “work up this company” and the workflow asks one round of questions — who they are and which decision the research feeds — then gathers evidence in parallel: the regulatory trail in SEC filings, the patent portfolio, the people, commercial signals and market context. Before a word of prose is written, every material number goes into a registry with its source and a confidence tier. The dossier follows a fixed template so companies stay comparable. The strongest case for failure is written next, and only then the opinion. If asked, the same registry becomes a working Excel model with bear, base and bull cases and a ranking of which assumption matters most.

Screen

One page, readable in ninety seconds: what the company is, what is known, what is not.

Dossier

The facts, in a fixed order, each claim tagged with where it came from. No judgment above the line.

Opinion

Behind a page break: the call, the two or three things it rests on, and what would change it.

The rules

  1. Numbers before prose. A number either has a row in the registry — value, source, confidence — or it does not go in. Guesses are allowed and labelled; relabelling a guess to look rigorous is the one move that destroys the document.
  2. Every claim carries its source. A company’s statement about its own market is evidence that the statement was made, not that it is true; it is recorded, checked independently, and the result noted.
  3. The bear case comes first. Assume it is three years later and the company has failed; explain why, in the strongest version, and where that was visible today. Only then write the opinion, with the specific facts that would flip it.
  4. Grade who stands behind every number. Separately from how a number was derived, it carries an independence grade: the company said it, a second source that isn’t independent of the company agrees, or a source with no stake in the answer would have said so if it were false. The rule that makes it worth doing is that no conclusion may rest on the company’s own account alone — and a third party validating data the company supplied does not count as independent.
  5. Every problem gets a disposition. Findings go in a register with a severity and an owner, and a column for what actually happens as a result — a diligence request, a condition, a walk-away trigger, or an explicit decision to live with it. A finding that never reaches a document binding somebody has changed nothing.
  6. Unknown stays unknown. Not “approximately”, and never a range invented to look thorough. Sometimes absence is the finding.

The method on a real company

To show the method rather than describe it, I pointed it at a company picked to fit a brief and nothing else: a California startup, Series A or earlier, building something physical rather than another AI product. It landed on Cosmic Buildings — a Los Angeles firm that parks a trailer of robot arms on your lot and frames the house there, selling into the Palisades fire rebuild. I had never heard of it. Everything below came from public records in a single day, and all three documents are here to download.

The one-page verdict

Call: Worth a first meeting. Not an investment on this evidence.

What it rests on

  1. The mechanism genuinely avoids the two costs that killed Katerra, Veev and Mighty Buildings — keeping a factory full, and trucking houses down a road. That is a real insight, not marketing.
  2. Twenty months after the fires that created its market, the record shows two finished backyard units and no finished house. In fairness that is partly the market: every builder in Los Angeles combined has rebuilt about 1.5% of what burned. But a competitor holds a certificate of occupancy on a factory-built rebuild, and this company does not.
  3. The prize is bounded. Framing is about a sixth of what a house costs to build, so even taking the claim of 80% off framing at face value, that is roughly 13% off the house — against a headline claim of 30%. And at these margins a home contributes about $39,000 against roughly $3.4M of overhead, putting break-even near 86 homes a year against 17 in the base case.

What would change my mind

  • One finished, occupied house, with its build time and final cost against the contract.
  • A contractor licence number that resolves on the state register.
  • Evidence of money raised since the $1.5M of March 2024.

Every number, tagged before any of it was written up

Growth is not the answer, which was a surprise. “Design clients who sign” is the number you would instinctively chase, and it ranks second from bottom. That is not a modelling quirk — it is the finding. At these margins each delivered home contributes about $39,000 against roughly $3.4M of fixed overhead, so break-even sits near 86 homes a year against 17 in the base case. Selling more of something delivered at a loss does not fix it.

Read the bottom row. “A hundred homes a year per microfactory” is the number in the press release, the trade coverage and the architecture magazines. In the model it changes the answer by nothing at all, because the robot’s capacity never binds — the company cannot sell houses fast enough to keep one of them busy. Adding microfactories makes the cash position worse. The impressive number and the important number are not the same number, and only building the thing shows you which is which.

What grading the evidence exposed

Splitting how a number was derived from who stands behind it changes what the document can claim. On this company, 18 of the 53 numbers are the company’s own account — the whole price list, every pipeline figure, the capacity and savings claims. So the headline result, break-even near 86 homes a year, is a conditional statement about the company’s own numbers rather than an independent measurement, and the workup now says so in that sentence. What is independently verified is the other half: the absent licence, the permit record, the two completions, the market data. The negative case rests on verified evidence; the quantified case rests on the company’s.

What the entry price would have to be

No round terms are public, so the model prices a hypothetical entry rather than reporting one — a $2M cheque at a $20M pre-money, diluting through two more rounds.

The equity is worth less than half the money going in; the preference is the only thing that pays. Getting to 3x needs either a software multiple on a construction business, or roughly six times the base-case revenue. Set against four companies in this category whose assets sold for a fraction of what they raised, neither is comfortable to underwrite.

Four things the checks turned up

A number that was not on the page

A counter reading 12 units commissioned sat in the site’s source code. Opening the page in a browser, it measures zero pixels tall — left in the markup, invisible to visitors. It nearly became evidence of an order book. It is a leftover.

A licence that is not on the register

The company states it is a licensed general contractor in California. The state register lists no licence under its name, none under its founder’s, and no pending application. It may sit under another entity — so the workup records this as not found, which is a different claim from not there.

A price rise wearing a flat price

The three headline prices have not moved since 2024. The floor areas behind them have: 395→380, 595→570, 750→720 sq ft. Same price, less house, which is a price increase per square foot presented as steady pricing.

A first attempt nobody wrote down

The founder ran this before. Formdwell, 2017, backed by Autodesk and Village Global, pitched a “mobile construction factory” — the same sentence. Its site is still up as a stub. No sale, no closure notice, no recorded ending. Bios list prior companies; outcomes are the part they leave out.

The four documents behind all of this are at the top of the page.

What it shows

  • Turning judgment about how analysis goes wrong into a repeatable process rather than a set of good intentions.
  • Skepticism built into the deliverable: provenance on every number, the case against before the case for, and confidence that tracks the evidence rather than conviction.
  • Packaging expertise as an AI workflow that another person, or a future version of me, can run the same way every time.

It works from public records only, so a private company’s footprint is often thin and a real workup contains honest guesses and unknowns; that is the point. The repository and the two earlier workups stay private — the one below is published in full so the method can be judged on its output rather than on its description.

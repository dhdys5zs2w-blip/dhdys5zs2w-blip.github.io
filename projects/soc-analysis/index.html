Can the physics of avalanches describe when a stock is about to get volatile?

Self-organized criticality is the idea that a system loaded slowly and disturbed at random — a sandpile, a forest — settles into a state where the next disturbance can be of any size. I researched whether that framework carries over to intraday markets, worked out what measurable signs of a “critical” state a price series would show, and directed the build of a dashboard that scores stocks on those signs and tests whether the score means anything.

The idea

In Per Bak’s sandpile, grains are added one at a time until the pile organises itself into a state where a single grain can trigger a slide of any size, and the sizes follow a power law: countless small slides, a few enormous ones, no typical size in between. The forest-fire model below is the same idea with trees and lightning. My question was whether a market behaves the same way — confidence building like trees growing, news arriving like lightning, crowded positions acting like connected trees — and, if so, whether the statistical fingerprints of a system near its critical point could be read off a stock’s recent trading before a volatility burst rather than after it.

The research pointed to several fingerprints that can each be measured on a series of returns: fat tails that follow a power law, long memory, a particular shape of noise spectrum, clusters of large moves, and the “critical slowing down” that ecology uses as an early-warning signal before a lake or a climate system tips. The hypothesis: a stock showing several of these at once is fragile, and if option prices do not yet reflect that, there is something to trade.

What the dashboard does

Pick a stock and it scores the last few hours of minute-by-minute trading for fragility, 0 to 100, on three time horizons at once, and labels the result safe, building, elevated or critical. It then compares what that score implies about coming volatility with what the market is pricing, and turns the gap into a signal: buy volatility, sell it, or stay out.

Does it actually work?

One stock over a few months proves nothing, so I ran it properly. Eight companies, one per sector, scored minute by minute across the year to August 2026 — about 250 sessions each, 1,996 company-days in all — with the list fixed before the run and chosen for sector coverage, not for how the names score. The question is simple: does a higher fragility score actually mean a more volatile next hour? Rather than grade the score against a pass mark I invented, I measured what it does and put it next to the obvious alternative. Throughout what follows, that alternative is the last hour’s volatility: how much the stock actually moved over the previous sixty minutes, and nothing else. No physics, no seven signatures, one line of arithmetic. If seven statistical signatures of criticality cannot beat that, they are not earning their place.

The results need JavaScript. The short version: the score does track the next hour’s volatility, but almost everything it knows is already available from the last hour’s volatility — simply how much the stock just moved — and once that is allowed for, what remains is indistinguishable from zero.

Does a higher score mean a more volatile next hour?

Each point is a tenth of the score, from the calmest readings to the most fragile. Height is how often a big volatility move followed, against how often one follows on an ordinary bar — so 1.0 means the reading told you nothing. A “big move” is defined as the top tenth of that stock’s own next-hour volatility, which is why the ordinary rate is one in ten by definition rather than by measurement. The dashed line is the identical measurement for the last hour’s volatility — how much the stock just moved, with no model in it — and it is the dashed line that climbs furthest at the right.

The answer

The score does track something real, and I expected to find less. Split it into tenths and the chance of a big volatility move climbs with it, from 0.87× the ordinary rate at the calmest readings to 1.24× at the most fragile. The model’s own labels order cleanly as well, from safe at 0.91× to critical at 1.21×, and all eight companies rank better than a coin flip.

The problem is what it is tracking. The last hour’s volatility on its own — no model, just how far the stock moved in the previous sixty minutes — explains about four times more of the next hour than the fragility score does, and separates calm from violent about equally well.

So the test that decides it is what the score adds once you already know the last hour’s volatility. The answer is almost exactly nothing: the correlation that survives is +0.010, indistinguishable from zero. Nearly everything the seven criticality signatures pick up is volatility clustering — the plain fact that turbulent periods run in streaks — which the simpler measure already captures, and captures better.

So the answer to the question at the top of this page is no. Not because the physics produced noise, but because it produced a more elaborate way of noticing something a rolling standard deviation already tells you. That is a real answer to the question I asked, and the test was built so it could come back this way.

Every figure here uses a strictly forward one-hour window and the same consensus score the chart above plots. Getting to it meant fixing two bugs that had been flattering the answer — a dependency that made every power-law fit fail silently, and a percentile function that gave tied values the top rank — and discarding an earlier measurement of mine that was taken against a window overlapping the past by half its length.

What it shows

  • Taking a framework from physics and turning it into a testable claim about markets, with a clear statement of what would prove it wrong.
  • Designing validation before trading: forward-looking checks and a costed backtest, not a fitted story.
  • Directing a large build with an AI coding tool from a research brief, and knowing which results to trust.
  • Running the test that could come back no, finding the bugs that were flattering the answer, and publishing the result anyway.

An exploratory research tool, not a trading system. The live app needs a market-data feed, so what runs on this page is the forest-fire model and one fixed historical window scored offline; the option-pricing backtest, which covers 29 trading days, stays in the app.

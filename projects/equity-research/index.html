A stock-ranking model, explained

Every evening, a frozen model ranks about 500 of the most‑traded US stocks.

This is what it does, how it did on ten years of data it had never seen, and how it has done live since July 1 — with the give-or-take left on every number.

01The idea in a minute

Prices in, a ranked list out, every evening.

Five steps, the same every night. None of them reads company accounts or the news: the model sees only prices and trading volumes — each stock's own, and its sector's and the market's to compare against.

  1. 500 The universe The 500 most-traded US stocks, re-picked every month by dollar volume — including companies that later disappeared, not only today's survivors (with one gap, described under the open questions).
  2. 19 Nineteen signals Measurements built from prices and volumes alone: how much a stock swings, where it sits against its 200-day average, how it has moved lately, and how it compares with its sector and the market.
  3. 2015 A frozen model A gradient-boosted model — many small decision trees, each correcting the last — learned from 2005–2015, once. It has not been retrained since.
  4. #1 A ranking Each evening it scores every stock and ranks them from favourite to least favourite.
  5. 39 The picks The top tenth, held in equal amounts. It only ever buys — it never bets on a stock falling (what traders call selling short). A stock joins in the top 10% and stays until it drops out of the top 30%, so it is usually held for several days and about a fifth of the list changes each day.

The trade you could actually make

  1. Day 1 · 4:00 pm ETThe market closes.
  2. Day 1 · about 6:30 pmThe model ranks every stock.
  3. Day 2 · 9:30 amBuy the picks at the open.
  4. Day 3 · 9:30 amSell at the open.

Every result on this page is measured that way — from the next morning's open to the open after — and against the average stock in the same sector, so a day when the whole market rises counts for nothing on its own. The picks do lean toward stocks that swing harder than the market, though, and how much of the result that lean explains is taken up under Does it work?

02How the model sees the market

It leans toward stocks that move a lot, and weighs where each one sits against its long-run trend.

Each dot is one of today's stocks. Across: how much it typically moves in a day. Up: how far its price sits above or below its 200-day average. The model's current picks are lit. Hover or tap a dot to see which stock it is; click to open it.

It does not simply buy the most volatile names. How much a stock swings is about a third of what the model pays attention to, and how far it sits from its 200-day average — a common gauge of a stock's long-run trend — about 28%. It weighs the two together, so a volatile stock can rank high or low depending on where it sits; the line under the chart says where today's picks sit.

03Does it work?

On a decade it had never seen, the picks beat their sector peers — by a little, and unevenly.

The model learned from 2005–2015. Everything from January 2016 to September 2026 was new to it: 2,684 trading days on which to check whether its picks did better than the average stock in their own sector.

+0.05%a day, on average

give or take 0.02%

That is +4.93 basis points (hundredths of a percent) a day, give or take 1.83 — about 2.7 times its give-or-take away from zero, so luck alone is an unlikely explanation, though not an impossible one. Added up over a year of trading days it comes to roughly +12%, before trading costs.

What that number is made of

Where the ranking works

Only the top tenth

Below the model's favourite tenth, its ranking does not sort returns: the second-best tenth is not reliably better than the middle. Everything rests on the top tenth, which is why the picks are only those names.

When it is earned

Overnight

The edge shows up between one day's close and the next morning's open. The ranking is ready on Day 1's evening, too late for that first night — but the edge carries into the next night too, from Day 2's close to Day 3's open, and the trade above holds the picks through it.

Where the size comes from

About half goes with a lean toward market-sensitive stocks

The picks lean toward stocks that swing further than the market when it moves. Roughly half of the average day goes with that lean, and what is left over is too small, so far, to tell apart from zero.

A second check points the same way. Take the picks' own returns — not measured against their sector — and remove the pull of the S&P 500, which they move about 1.6 times as much as: about +0.014% a day (+1.42 basis points) is left, again too small to tell apart from zero.

So it is not yet shown that the model adds anything beyond that lean. The headline above includes it.

What trading costs

Costs eat into it quickly

Each stock swapped in costs a round trip — buying it, and later selling it — and about a fifth of the picks are swapped each day. At a round-trip cost of 2.5, 10 or 20 basis points, roughly +4.4, +2.9 or +0.9 basis points a day is left, each give or take about 1.8, as the headline is. At 20, what is left cannot be told apart from zero.

How long until we know

57 of about 250 live trading days

About a year of trading days — roughly 250 — is what the live record needs before it can say much either way, and even then it will catch a large miss more easily than it can confirm an edge as small as the tested one. 193 to go: at one a weekday, about 9 more months.

06The guardrails

Built to be hard to fool, itself included.

Most models that look good on history do so because someone kept adjusting them until they did. Four habits stand in the way of that here.

  1. Rules first, results second.Every test's pass-or-fail rule was written down before the test ran, so no threshold was chosen after seeing the answer.
  2. A test on shuffled data.The whole pipeline was run on data whose outcomes had been shuffled, where there is nothing to find — and it found nothing. A pipeline that finds patterns in noise would have shown it there.
  3. Never retuned.The model has not been adjusted on recent data. Every number on this page comes from the one model, trained once.
  4. Failures stay on the record.Ideas that did not work are kept and written up, not quietly deleted — a few of them are listed below.

07What we don't know yet

Four open questions.

  • Whether live will match the test.The live record is short. About a year of live days is needed before it can say much either way about the tested edge.
  • What trading really costs.No real trades have been logged yet, so the cost of trading at the open is an estimate, not a measurement.
  • A gap in the test data.Stocks without a current sector label — about 8% of the tested stock-days — were left out of the test. What that did to the number has not been measured yet.
  • Why it varies so much by year.The edge has been uneven: below zero in 2022, large in 2020 and 2025. A decade is not many years to learn a pattern like that from.

08Ideas that didn't pan out

Tried, measured, set aside.

Six ideas that were tried or weighed, and set aside
  • Finer ways to use the rankingWeighting names by score, concentrating the picks, splitting them into slices: the top tenth is the only part of the ranking that works, so there was nothing finer to use.
  • Leaning the training toward recent yearsDid not help — if anything it did slightly worse. (A second version, trained on data up to mid-2025 and on the trade you could actually make, is tracked beside this one each night without being traded; it is not part of any number on this page.)
  • Switching on or sizing by market moodTurning the picks up or down with how volatile the market is, or how far apart stocks' moves are, was not reliable.
  • An overnight-only versionHolding only from the close to the next open earns less than it would cost to trade that often.
  • The model against a simpler rankingComparing the picks with a plain sort on one measure, such as how strongly each stock moves with the market: too few days to tell the two apart. That comparison now builds up night by night instead.
  • Betting against its least-favourite stocksSelling short the bottom of the list was not pursued: below the top tenth the ranking does not sort returns, so the bottom is not reliably worse than the middle.

Ask a question of ten years of stock prices. Pick which stocks, on which days — by the model's ranking, earnings reports, sector, price patterns or the calendar — and see what their returns looked like next, set against every other stock on the same days.

Looking is not testing. Every question you try is another draw, and among enough of them one will look good by chance — the page counts your looks and says how large a chance result would be. Something found here is a hypothesis to pre-register before it is run, not evidence.

1 Which stock-days?

A stock-day is one stock on one trading day — call that day day 0. With no conditions you get every stock on every day; each condition keeps (or leaves out) the stock-days that match it.

    How to read this

    Each of your stock-days has one return: the stored target read a set number of trading days after day 0. Held 1 day and starting 1 day after, it is the close of day 1 to the close of day 2. Against its sector is the stock's return minus its sector's median that day (the model's own target); raw is the plain return; against the market takes out the market in proportion to the stock's beta.

    The unit is the day. Each day's average over your stock-days is set against the average of every other stock with the same return that day, and the figures average over days — a day with one matching stock counts as much as a day with fifty. The ± is a standard error from the platform's block bootstrap over those days (blocks of 5, or of the holding period if longer, because a 5-day return overlaps the next four days'). t is the average divided by its standard error: how many standard errors it sits from zero. The median, the share above zero and the histogram pool the stock-days and carry no standard error.

    A window that starts at day 0's own close cannot be traded when the question uses something computed from that close — the model's rankings, a price pattern, the volume rank — and the page says so. Earnings dates are the reported ones; most are announced weeks ahead, but choosing on a report that had not happened yet is hindsight, and choosing on whether it beat is knowing the outcome.

    backtest The frozen model scored on dates it never traded, after the fact. A description of the holdout, not evidence — only live days are evidence.
